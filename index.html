RSPCA Animal Welfare in Australia · 2024-25

Who Speaks for the Animals?

A kitten found shivering in a drain. A kangaroo hit on a country road. A dog handed over by a family who could no longer cope. Every animal that reaches the RSPCA arrives with a story, and last year more than 77,000 of those stories began at a shelter door somewhere in Australia.

77,464 animals who needed the RSPCA in a single year
67,237 cruelty complaints followed up by RSPCA inspectors
381 cruelty cases that ended in court
For Malaysian readers: Australia is made up of six states and two territories, and each one has its own RSPCA, much like SPCA Selangor and SPCA Penang work independently here at home. The figures cover July 2024 to June 2025.

01

77,000 lives in a single year

Most of us picture the RSPCA as a place where cats and dogs wait for a family. They are a big part of the story: together, more than half of every animal that came through the doors.

But the largest group is the one we rarely think about. Wildlife. Possums, birds, kangaroos and koalas, many of them hurt by cars, storms or bushfire. More than one in three animals the RSPCA cared for last year was wild.

Each square stands for 1 in every 100 animals received in 2024-25.

02

Fewer animals arriving, but a harder road for dogs

Five years ago, the RSPCA took in more than 103,000 animals. Last year it was closer to 77,000, a quarter fewer. Cats saw the biggest change, falling by 40%, in the same years the RSPCA campaigned for desexing and for keeping pet cats safely at home.

Dogs tell a quieter, sadder story. Fewer are arriving, yet more of those who do never leave. In 2020-21, about 1 in 9 dogs in RSPCA care was put to sleep. Last year, it was about 1 in 6.

03

Where an animal lives shapes what happens to it

Each state runs its own RSPCA, and the pressure on every one of them looks different. In some places, neighbours speak up for animals often. In others, a single shelter is asked to carry far more than its share.

To keep the comparison fair, the first map counts cruelty complaints for every 10,000 people, so a state does not look worse simply because more people live there.

04

Why some animals never go home

Most dogs and cats who enter RSPCA care do leave alive, adopted by a new family or reunited with their own. But their chances depend heavily on where they land.

In the ACT, about 9 in 10 walk back out the door. In the Northern Territory and Western Australia, it is closer to 2 in 3.

The Northern Territory's shelters face the heaviest pressure in the country. They take in more dogs and cats for every person than anywhere else, and they see the fewest leave alive. When so many animals arrive at once, there are only so many homes to go around.

Even the reasons behind each loss differ. Dogs are most often put to sleep because of behaviour: fear or aggression that makes them unsafe to rehome. In the Northern Territory, that was the reason for 7 in every 10 dogs lost. Cats are more often lost to illness or injury that cannot be treated.

Behind every percentage on this page is an animal that simply ran out of options.
